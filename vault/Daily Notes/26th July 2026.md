## 26th July 2026

Set up the Claude Code OS. Bijoy is now running it — vault, memory system, hooks, rule bank, and the daily morning content ritual are all configured and in the repo.

Worth noting: the whole system was built in a cloud session rather than on the Mac, so it lives in the M.AI repo with a one-command installer instead of being installed directly. Two portability bugs in the shipped setup scripts got fixed along the way, both of which would have broken the install on a stock Mac.

Direction set for the solo AI business: first job is picking the opening offer and doing the market survey behind it, before anything gets built.

Then the direction changed. Instead of the outreach tool that was scoped first, the work moved to AlarmX — an Android alarm app that pays people to wake up on time.

The important thing that came out of it was the arithmetic. The concept planned to pay up to ₹95 per user a month, and modelling the ad revenue and survey resale against that showed roughly ₹23 coming in. At a hundred thousand users that gap is about ₹39 lakh a month going out the door. Everything else that looked like a design problem turned out to be a symptom of it: the plan to let people build up ₹500 and then release it slowly existed only because the payouts were never affordable in the first place. Cap what people can earn, be honest about it, and pay it in full, and the problem stops being a trust problem.

Two other things got fixed on principle rather than economics. The alarm used to give up after ten seconds, which meant the app itself could be the reason someone was late for work — now it rings until it's dismissed and the ten seconds only decides whether you get paid. And flagged accounts were going to be shown a fake error message; they'll be told they're under review instead, because every fraud system catches innocent people and those people need somewhere to go.

Built the economics model, the spec, and a working prototype with a test suite behind it. Testing turned up a real conflict nobody had spotted: a ₹20 monthly cap against a ₹30 minimum withdrawal means a new user cannot get paid at all in their first month, which undercuts the entire honesty argument.

Bijoy settled it the right way — first payout drops to ₹10, then ₹30 after. Modelling that honestly showed it is not free: when the threshold is lower, fewer people quit before ever cashing out, so more of what is owed actually gets paid, and the affordable monthly cap falls from about ₹21 to ₹16. Launch cap is now ₹15. The thing that makes it worth doing is a change in how it is counted rather than a change in the number: that first ₹10 is really the cost of proving the app pays, and proving it costs less than half of what buying an install through an ad would. It also needs a guard, because a small payout to every new account is an invitation — so it only unlocks after seven separate mornings with a real alarm, which is a week no bot can hurry.

Then the product got pointed somewhere specific. It is for exam students now — NEET, JEE, UPSC — because getting up at five to study is a thing people already want to do, so the money is a bonus rather than the whole argument. And it opens in Hinglish, which is how the people it is for actually read.

The part most likely to kill it turns out to be nothing to do with rewards at all: phones like Redmi and Realme aggressively shut background apps down, so the alarm simply would not ring for a large share of the intended users. That now has its own setup walkthrough, and testing it on real handsets is a task rather than an assumption.

Last thing today was Bijoy's idea and it is the best one of the day. A capped app cannot buy attention with money, so it has to borrow a habit that already exists. Bengali panjika and the Tamil daily calendar get opened every single morning by millions of households, so the app now carries a calendar and a home screen widget. The calendar is the reason to look, and the alarm, the pot and the exam countdown are simply what is on the screen while looking. Nothing has to nag anyone.

It quietly fixed a problem nobody had named. The calendar crowd is older and household, the exam crowd is young, and those felt like two different products. One widget holding both the tithi and the days left to NEET turns out to serve both.

The rule was that none of it could be copied. Both reference sites are copyrighted and block automated requests anyway, so every date is calculated from the positions of the sun and the moon. That took most of the effort and it caught real mistakes: the app first showed the wrong day for the Bengali new year, and Pongal was a day out, because the two traditions start their day by different rules and one of them depends on whether the sun crosses before sunset. It now gets Puthandu, Poila Boishakh and Pongal right across two different years, and the two traditions disagree with each other on screen exactly the way they disagree in real life.

Bengali and Tamil became proper languages in the app rather than a label on an English screen, which meant the tithi and nakshatra names had to change script too. Five languages now.

The honest part: this is the one feature where being wrong is worse than being absent, because people plan fasts and rituals on it. So checking sixty dates against a printed panjika and a printed Tamil calendar is now a blocker before release, and it has not been done. The astronomy licence has to be bought before anything ships too, because the free version would force the whole app open including the anti-cheating code.

Then Bijoy sent two pieces of other people's code and asked whether they could be used to check the calendar. They could, but not in the way it first looked. Both turned out to implement the Bangladesh calendar rather than the West Bengal one, and those are genuinely different things: Bangladesh fixed its month lengths so the new year always falls on the fourteenth of April, while the Bengal panjika follows the sun and usually lands on the fifteenth. That is a real difference people live with, not a rounding error.

Two useful things came out of it. The first is proper outside proof. Both pieces of code agreed with each other on every one of fourteen hundred and sixty one days, which makes them worth trusting, and the app now reproduces them exactly. It also confirmed the app gets right the one day everyone notices: Bengal kept the new year on the fifteenth of April last year while Bangladesh kept it on the fourteenth, and the app says so on its own.

The second is the more valuable one, and it was a gap nobody had spotted. The app was only ever going to show the Bengal dates, so anyone in Dhaka would have been given the wrong day for the biggest day of their year. There are more Bengali speakers in Bangladesh than in West Bengal. That is now a third calendar in the app, and it was the cheapest thing built all day because it needs no astronomy at all, just a table.

Worth being clear about what this did not prove. Neither piece of code calculates tithi or nakshatra, so none of that got checked, and the printed calendar cross-check is still sitting there as a blocker.

Last exchange of the day was Bijoy pushing back, correctly, that the money does not come from surveys but from advertising. He is right and the model already said so: ads are sixty one percent of the base case. But checking it properly showed something he had not expected. Take the survey line out entirely and the cautious case goes negative, because the advertising on its own does not even cover the cost of paying people out. Having several revenue lines is not the same as being safe. It only moves the risk from a price he can haggle over to an advertising rate Google decides.

He also wanted an advert before the maths problem. That one had to be turned down, and the way to settle it was to price it rather than argue about it. The maths problem is how you switch the alarm off, so an advert there means the app stands between somebody and a five in the morning alarm, which is exactly the thing that was fixed first. It is also close to the worst case a Play reviewer could be shown. And it earns seventy three paise a month. That number ended the discussion in one line.

The better answer was sitting right there. One more of the videos people choose to watch is worth nearly four times one more forced advert, because the paid rate on those is much higher. So the video count went from three to four, the ceiling rose from about sixteen to about nineteen rupees, and the actual payout stays at fifteen. The extra is kept back as margin, because four videos a day is still a guess and spending money against a guess is how the ninety five rupee version happened.

The rule about where adverts can and cannot go is now written into the spec and, more usefully, into the tests. Every advert space in the app is tagged, and the test simply counts how many are on the alarm screen. It has to be zero.

Then the whole reward system got rebuilt, and this is the change that matters most.

Bijoy asked for three things: something that makes people open the app every day, something that makes them feel they earned money by the evening, and above all never losing money. The third one settled the other two.

Every version so far had failed the same way. Ninety five rupees a month, fifty paise a maths, two rupees a set. Each time a number was picked first and then defended, and each time it lost money the moment the advertising rate moved. Advertising income is set by Google, not by us, so a fixed payment against it is just a loss waiting for a bad month.

So the payment stopped being a number. People are now paid a share of advertising money that has already arrived. Half of it, rising to sixty percent for anyone who keeps a long streak, and seventy percent until their first ten rupees so they find out quickly that it is real. Nothing is credited unless the server has confirmed the advert genuinely paid. If the rate halves, payments halve the same day. If no advert loads, nothing is owed and nothing is paid. Losing money on rewards is no longer unlikely, it is not possible.

The monthly limit is gone too, and that turned out to be a hidden problem rather than a safeguard. A monthly limit means whoever hits it earns nothing for the rest of the month, which is the exact opposite of getting people to open the app daily. It is now a daily limit on how many adverts count, which stops abuse without ever making the app go quiet.

The daytime loop Bijoy wanted is built: five sets of four questions, one video after every two, locked until the morning alarm is done so the money still traces back to actually getting up. The sets open in three batches through the day, so one visit becomes three.

The best part is the evening. The app now closes the day by showing what was earned and how it was worked out: how many adverts, what share, how many rupees. Every app in this category hides that, which is precisely why nobody trusts any of them. We can show it because ours is honest, and the number on screen is calculated from the record rather than typed in.

The guarantee is also built into the tooling rather than written in a document. The spreadsheet refuses to be generated at all if any scenario, at any share level, at a quarter of the assumed advertising rate, would lose money. Thirty six combinations checked every time, and it was deliberately broken once to prove the check works.
