## 26th July 2026

Set up the Claude Code OS. Bijoy is now running it — vault, memory system, hooks, rule bank, and the daily morning content ritual are all configured and in the repo.

Worth noting: the whole system was built in a cloud session rather than on the Mac, so it lives in the M.AI repo with a one-command installer instead of being installed directly. Two portability bugs in the shipped setup scripts got fixed along the way, both of which would have broken the install on a stock Mac.

Direction set for the solo AI business: first job is picking the opening offer and doing the market survey behind it, before anything gets built.

Then the direction changed. Instead of the outreach tool that was scoped first, the work moved to AlarmX — an Android alarm app that pays people to wake up on time.

The important thing that came out of it was the arithmetic. The concept planned to pay up to ₹95 per user a month, and modelling the ad revenue and survey resale against that showed roughly ₹23 coming in. At a hundred thousand users that gap is about ₹39 lakh a month going out the door. Everything else that looked like a design problem turned out to be a symptom of it: the plan to let people build up ₹500 and then release it slowly existed only because the payouts were never affordable in the first place. Cap what people can earn, be honest about it, and pay it in full, and the problem stops being a trust problem.

Two other things got fixed on principle rather than economics. The alarm used to give up after ten seconds, which meant the app itself could be the reason someone was late for work — now it rings until it's dismissed and the ten seconds only decides whether you get paid. And flagged accounts were going to be shown a fake error message; they'll be told they're under review instead, because every fraud system catches innocent people and those people need somewhere to go.

Built the economics model, the spec, and a working prototype with a test suite behind it. Testing turned up a real conflict nobody had spotted: a ₹20 monthly cap against a ₹30 minimum withdrawal means a new user cannot get paid at all in their first month, which undercuts the entire honesty argument.

Bijoy settled it the right way — first payout drops to ₹10, then ₹30 after. Modelling that honestly showed it is not free: when the threshold is lower, fewer people quit before ever cashing out, so more of what is owed actually gets paid, and the affordable monthly cap falls from about ₹21 to ₹16. Launch cap is now ₹15. The thing that makes it worth doing is a change in how it is counted rather than a change in the number: that first ₹10 is really the cost of proving the app pays, and proving it costs less than half of what buying an install through an ad would. It also needs a guard, because a small payout to every new account is an invitation — so it only unlocks after seven separate mornings with a real alarm, which is a week no bot can hurry.

Then the product got pointed somewhere specific. It is for exam students now — NEET, JEE, UPSC — because getting up at five to study is a thing people already want to do, so the money is a bonus rather than the whole argument. And it opens in Hinglish, which is how the people it is for actually read.

The part most likely to kill it turns out to be nothing to do with rewards at all: phones like Redmi and Realme aggressively shut background apps down, so the alarm simply would not ring for a large share of the intended users. That now has its own setup walkthrough, and testing it on real handsets is a task rather than an assumption.
